\begin{abstract}
We present a novel method which combines the invertibility of Germano's filter with generative artificial intelligence to accelerate edge plasma turbulence simulations. Rather than learning the mapping from coarse LES fields to fine DNS, the proposed method learns the mapping from coarse LES fields to Germano Differential-filtered DNS fields. We then use the inverse of Germano's filter to reconstruct the fine-scale DNS fields and hence calculate exactly the nonlinear terms in the governing equations, or the subgrid-scale terms in the LES.

The method is tested on the Hasegawa--Wakatani and modified Hasegawa--Wakatani plasma turbulence models at $256^2$ and $512^2$ DNS resolutions using 2, 4 and 8 times smaller LES grids in each direction. We compare the generative model with DNS and conventional LES diagnostics, including energy, enstrophy, spectra, probability distributions, poloidal and radial fluxes and computational cost. The supplied U-Net validation results show qualitative agreement in several integral and spectral diagnostics, but also delayed transients and excess late-time enstrophy in the modified $256^2$ case. The completed HW runs show reductions in MPI task-time per unit simulated time by factors of 5-185; a wall-clock speedup of 1.45 is obtained for the $64^2$ LES referenced to the $512^2$ DNS.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:Introduction}

Full scales solved simulations of plasma edge turbulence via Direct Numerical Simulation (DNS) are often prohibitive, even on current modern exascale hardware. The algorithm complexity and the stiffness of the solving matrix are often the main culprits and simplified models are often used as first alternative. On the other hand, Large Eddy Simulations (LES) are much faster, being run on a coarser resolution altogether, but an ad-hoc modelling of the sub-grid scales is required. In strongly magnetized plasma it is notoriously very challenging to find correct models, being the energy injected at small length and, via inverse cascade, propagated to large scales, as described on the work on 2D turbulence by \citet{kraichnan_inertial_1967}.

The 2D Hasegawa--Wakatani (HW) model \citep{hasegawa_self-organization_1987} couples plasma-density and electrostatic-potential fluctuations to describe resistive drift-wave turbulence. To account for zonal-flow dynamics, the modified Hasegawa--Wakatani (mHW) model excludes the zonal components from the resistive coupling \citep{numata_bifurcation_2007}. This reflects the absence of a parallel-electron response for zonal modes, which are constant on a flux surface. Removing their direct resistive damping allows zonal shear flows to develop and, depending on the parameters, suppress drift-wave turbulence. The HW and mHW models therefore provide complementary tests of a learned LES closure in regimes with different interactions between turbulent fluctuations and zonal flows.

Deconvolution operators for LES have been proposed in the past as Approximate Deconvolution Methods (ADM) by \citet{stolz_approximate_1999}. However, recent interest in deep neural networks for computational fluid dynamics \citep{wang_recent_2024} for a good review of the work done in the last five years) showed a Cambrian explosion of these methodologies, ranging from mixed physics-data-driven approaches to more exotic pure data-driven implementations where the full modeling of the equations is completely replaced by foundation models. Across the generative AI techniques which could be used as a deconvolution operator for LES or, more generally, for the reconstruction of fine detailed images from a coarser level (known as the super-resolution problem), Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs, \citealp{goodfellow_generative_2014}) are playing a major role, together with Variational AutoEncoders \citep{kingma_auto-encoding_2022} and Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models \citep{ho_denoising_2020}. Each of the three main methodologies has pros and cons, mainly on training robustness, accuracy and speed. In our previous work \citep{castagna_stylegan_2024} we used StyleGAN \citep{karras_style-based_2019}, a model developed by NVIDIA for face synthesis, as a deconvolution operator for 2D turbulent flows. The algorithm used, named Style Eddy Simulation (StylES), showed a factor $10\times$ speedup vs single core DNS for $1024^2$ resolution. Similar applications of GANs to simulation of turbulent flows can be found in the work of \citet{kim_deep_2020}, \citet{kim_unsupervised_2021} and \citet{bode_using_2021}.

In the following paper we improved our previous work on StylES \citep{castagna_stylegan_2024}, replacing the full StyleGAN architecture with a simpler U-Net based architecture, but moving the training target from the fully developed DNS fields to the Germano's filtered LES fields without dowsampling. 
The idea is that the generator learns the mapping from coarse LES fields to fine LES resolution fields, and then reconstruct the DNS details using the inverse of Germano's filter. This approach allows for: a) a much faster super-resolution and hence time integration and b) simplify drastically the training process, notoriously difficult for GANs.

The paper continues as follows: Sections~\ref{sec:PhysicalModel} introudce the physical model and Germano's filter in LES. Section~\ref{sec:Numerical Implementation} section explains the U-Net based architecture and the modifications needed to have a stable time integration. Moreover, details on the training and reconstruction methodology are provided. Section~\ref{sec:Results} presents the results and discussion on the performance. Finally, Section~\ref{sec:Conclusions} summarizes the findings and proposes future work, which will be mainly moving towards a full 3D implementation of the proposed methodology.

The code used to generate the results is available on the U-Net-LES repositories at \url{https://github.com/stfc/U-Net-LES} and the model weights are available on Zenodo at \url{https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.22973917}.

\section{Physical model}\label{sec:PhysicalModel}

\subsection{Hasegawa-Wakatani model}
In a tokamak device, one of the first simplified model which describes the transport of turbulence in the scrape-off layer is described by Hasegawa-Wakatani (HW) equations \citep{hasegawa_self-organization_1987}. This consists of a perpendicular slab to the toroidal magnetic field, with radial coordinate $x$ and poloidal coordinate $y$. The time evolution of the normalized density perturbation $n$, electrostatic potential $\phi$ and vorticity $\zeta$ are given by the two phase fluid magnetohydrodynamic equations:
\begin{align}
    \frac{\partial\zeta}{\partial t}+\{\phi,\zeta\}
    &=\alpha(\phi-n)-D_\zeta\nabla_\perp^4\zeta,
    \label{eq:hw_vorticity}\\
    \frac{\partial n}{\partial t}+\{\phi,n\}
    &=\alpha(\phi-n)-\kappa\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial y}
      -D_n\nabla_\perp^4 n.
    \label{eq:hw_density}
\end{align}
The potential follows from:
\begin{equation}
    \nabla_\perp^2\phi=\zeta,
    \qquad
    \{a,b\}=\partial_xa\,\partial_yb-\partial_ya\,\partial_xb.
    \label{eq:hw_poisson}
\end{equation}
The bracket describes $\mathbf{E}\times\mathbf{B}$ advection. The constant $\alpha$ represents the parallel-electron response, while $\kappa=-\partial_x\ln n_0$ specifies the background density-gradient drive. Fourth-order dissipation removes small-scale fluctuations, and it is known as hyperviscosity as can be rewritten in different forms, according to the numerical scheme used and the wavenumber cut-off.

For $\alpha=0$, vorticity evolves independently and the incompressible hydrodynamic equations are recovered. For large $\alpha$, the standard HW model approaches $n=\phi$. Neglecting dissipation, subtraction of the density equation gives the Hasegawa--Mima limit:
\begin{equation}
    q=\nabla_\perp^2\phi-\phi,
    \qquad
    \partial_tq+\{\phi,q\}=\kappa\partial_y\phi.
    \label{eq:hm_limit}
\end{equation}

The modified HW model removes zonal components from the coupling \citep{numata_bifurcation_2007}.
By setting:
\begin{equation}
    \langle f\rangle_y=\frac{1}{L_y}\int_0^{L_y}f(x,y,t)\,dy,
    \qquad f'=f-\langle f\rangle_y.
    \label{eq:zonal_decomposition}
\end{equation}
the equations become:
\begin{align}
    \frac{\partial\zeta}{\partial t}+\{\phi,\zeta\}
    &=\alpha(\phi'-n')-D_\zeta\nabla_\perp^4\zeta,
    \label{eq:mhw_vorticity}\\
    \frac{\partial n}{\partial t}+\{\phi,n\}
    &=\alpha(\phi'-n')-\kappa\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial y}
      -D_n\nabla_\perp^4 n.
    \label{eq:mhw_density}
\end{align}
which introduces zonal modes without parallel-electron response which. Excluding their direct resistive damping permits zonal shear flows that can suppress turbulence \citep{numata_bifurcation_2007}.

We use primes for non-zonal fluctuations and reserve tildes for LES-filtered fields. This distinction matters because the zonal average and the LES filter serve different purposes: the former defines the physical coupling, while the latter introduces the unresolved nonlinear terms discussed below.

\subsection{Germano Differential-filtered LES formulation}\label{sec:GermanoDifferentialFilter}

\citet{germano_differential_1986} introduced spatial filters whose inverse is a differential operator. For the isotropic case, define:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{H}_\ell=I-\ell^2\nabla_\perp^2,
    \qquad \mathcal{F}_\ell=\mathcal{H}_\ell^{-1},
    \qquad \widetilde f=\mathcal{F}_\ell f.
    \label{eq:germano_filter}
\end{equation}
Thus filtering amounts to solving a Helmholtz problem, whereas its formal inverse is local:
\begin{equation}
    (I-\ell^2\nabla_\perp^2)\widetilde f=f,
    \qquad f=\widetilde f-\ell^2\nabla_\perp^2\widetilde f.
    \label{eq:germano_inverse}
\end{equation}
Here $\ell$, usually indicated with $\alpha$, is a spatial filtering length, distinct from the HW adiabaticity parameter $\alpha$. The inverse requires boundary conditions consistent with the forward filter; the present implementation uses periodic Fourier transforms.

In Germano's unbounded three-dimensional construction, the corresponding normalized convolution kernel is $G_\ell(r)=\exp(-r/\ell)/(4\pi\ell^2r)$. For our two-dimensional periodic domain, the filter is instead specified by its Fourier multiplier:
\begin{equation}
    \widehat{\widetilde f}(\mathbf{k})
    =\frac{\widehat f(\mathbf{k})}{1+\ell^2|\mathbf{k}|^2},
    \qquad
    \widehat f(\mathbf{k})
    =(1+\ell^2|\mathbf{k}|^2)\widehat{\widetilde f}(\mathbf{k}).
    \label{eq:germano_transfer}
\end{equation}
Hats denote Fourier coefficients. The zero mode is preserved, and short wavelengths are attenuated algebraically without a sharp spectral cutoff. This differs from Gaussian filtering, whose attenuation is exponential in squared wavenumber. Figure~\ref{fig:hw_filter_spectra} compares the unfiltered, Gaussian-filtered and Germano-filtered energy spectra for an HW snapshot, illustrating their different attenuation at high wavenumbers. Germano also gives the constant-coefficient anisotropic extension $I-A_{ij}\partial_i\partial_j$, with positive-definite $A_{ij}$ and multiplier $(1+A_{ij}k_i k_j)^{-1}$; we use the isotropic choice $A_{ij}=\ell^2\delta_{ij}$.

\caption{Energy spectra for the HW run-0 snapshot at $t=501$ on a $256^2$ mesh, comparing the unfiltered field with Gaussian and Germano filtering. Both filters attenuate the high-wavenumber spectrum; the Gaussian-filtered spectrum falls more steeply than the Germano-filtered spectrum in the plotted comparison.}
\label{fig:hw_filter_spectra}
\end{figure}


For constant $\ell$ on the periodic domain, filtering is linear and commutes with spatial derivatives and the time derivative, i.e. satisfy the criteria for LES filtering of lineary, conservation and commutation \citep{sagaut_large_2006}. It also commutes with the poloidal average defining the mHW coupling. These properties justify filtering the linear terms directly; they should not be assumed for spatially varying filter widths or incompatible boundary treatments. Applying $\mathcal{F}_\ell$ to Eqs.~\eqref{eq:hw_vorticity}--\eqref{eq:hw_density} yields
\begin{align}
    \partial_t\widetilde\zeta+\{\widetilde\phi,\widetilde\zeta\}
    &=\alpha(\widetilde\phi-\widetilde n)
      -\tau_{\mathrm{SGS},\zeta}-D_\zeta\nabla_\perp^4\widetilde\zeta,
    \label{eq:filtered_hw_vorticity}\\
    \partial_t\widetilde n+\{\widetilde\phi,\widetilde n\}
    &=\alpha(\widetilde\phi-\widetilde n)
      -\tau_{\mathrm{SGS},n}-\kappa\partial_y\widetilde\phi
      -D_n\nabla_\perp^4\widetilde n.
    \label{eq:filtered_hw_density}
\end{align}
For mHW, replace the coupling difference by $\widetilde\phi'-\widetilde n'$, where $\widetilde f'=\widetilde f-\langle\widetilde f\rangle_y$. The exact nonlinear residual is
\begin{equation}
    \tau_{\mathrm{SGS},f}
    =\mathcal{F}_\ell\{\phi,f\}-\{\widetilde\phi,\widetilde f\},
    \qquad f\in\{n,\zeta\}.
    \label{eq:sgs_bracket}
\end{equation}
which is the terms we are going to calculate using the learned reconstruction.

A central result of \citet{germano_differential_1986} is that the differential inverse gives exact expressions for filtered correlations. For two smooth scalar fields $a$ and $b$, define $C_\ell(a,b)=\mathcal{F}_\ell(ab)-\widetilde a\widetilde b$. The product rule and Eq.~\eqref{eq:germano_inverse} give
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{H}_\ell C_\ell(a,b)
    =2\ell^2\nabla_\perp\widetilde a\cdot\nabla_\perp\widetilde b
     +\ell^4(\nabla_\perp^2\widetilde a)(\nabla_\perp^2\widetilde b).
    \label{eq:germano_correlation}
\end{equation}
Consequently, the exact correlation is the forward filter of the right-hand side, rather than just its leading gradient term. Applied to the two products in the Poisson bracket, this identity gives
\begin{equation}
    \tau_{\mathrm{SGS},f}
    =C_\ell(\partial_x\phi,\partial_y f)
     -C_\ell(\partial_y\phi,\partial_x f).
\end{equation}
Equivalently, the nonlinear term can be evaluated by reconstructing both fields:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{F}_\ell\{\phi,f\}
    =\mathcal{F}_\ell\{
       \mathcal{H}_\ell\widetilde\phi,
       \mathcal{H}_\ell\widetilde f\}.
    \label{eq:germano_bracket_inverse}
\end{equation}
These are continuous identities, not a closure assumption or a guarantee of exact reconstruction from coarse samples. As Germano emphasizes, an exactly inverted filtered system is formally a change of dependent variables; numerical resolution still limits the information retained.

In particular, the physical filter and the downsampling to the LES mesh are separate operations. Writing $\mathcal{R}_h$ for Fourier downsampling to that mesh, the network input is $(n,\phi)_{\mathrm{LES}}=\mathcal{R}_h\mathcal{F}_\ell(n,\phi)_{\mathrm{DNS}}$. Downsampling discards modes that the differential inverse alone cannot restore. Our learned reconstruction supplies an estimate of the filtered field on the fine mesh before inversion:
\begin{equation}
    (n,\phi)_{\mathrm{LES}}
    \xrightarrow{\mathcal{N}_\theta}
    (\widetilde n^{\mathrm{pred}},\widetilde\phi^{\mathrm{pred}})_{\mathrm{DNS}}
    \xrightarrow{\mathcal{H}_\ell}
    (n^{\mathrm{rec}},\phi^{\mathrm{rec}})_{\mathrm{DNS}}.
    \label{eq:germano_learning_path}
\end{equation}
The inverse multiplier grows as $|\mathbf{k}|^2$, amplifying high-wavenumber prediction errors. The implementation therefore permits spectral regularization before or after inversion; when enabled, it changes the reconstruction from the exact inverse in Eq.~\eqref{eq:germano_inverse}. Vorticity is derived consistently as $\zeta^{\mathrm{rec}}=\nabla_\perp^2\phi^{\mathrm{rec}}$. The reconstructed nonlinear terms are then filtered and restricted,
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{F}_\ell\{\phi,f\}
    =\mathcal{R}_h\mathcal{F}_\ell
      \{\phi^{\mathrm{rec}},f^{\mathrm{rec}}\},
      \qquad f\in\{n,\zeta\}.
\end{equation}
providing the coarse solver with an approximation to the filtered fine-grid advection. This separates the analytically defined Germano inverse from the learned recovery of information lost through mesh downsampling.

\subsection{NN-Architecture}\label{sec:NN-Architecture}
We use a variation of the U-Net architecture \citep{ronneberger_u-net_2015} for the generative model. However, as the input is already at the LES coarse resolution, we have an asymmetric architecture with a small encoder followed by a decoder to upsample the input to the fine DNS resolution. The reconstruction configuration described here is the HW training run with a $256^2$ DNS mesh and a $32^2$ LES mesh. The settings below refer to this run; they are not assumed to apply to every resolution or modified-HW case.

The three coarse input channels are density, potential and vorticity, denoted by $\mathbf{U}=(n,\phi,\zeta)$. The network predicts two fine-grid filtered fields, $\widetilde n_\theta$ and $\widetilde\phi_\theta$, being the vorticity and Laplacian of the potential fields $\zeta=\nabla_\perp^2\phi$. With a base channel count of 24, the encoder begins with 96 feature channels on the $32^2$ input mesh and uses a 192-channel bottleneck on a $16^2$ mesh. A skip-connected decoder returns to $32^2$, followed by periodic bilinear upsampling stages reaching $64^2$, $128^2$ and $256^2$. Circular convolution padding and periodic interpolation preserve the domain topology. The model has 1,670,738 trainable parameters and no global input skip connection. Figure~\ref{fig:direct_unet_architecture} summarizes the network and its differentiable reconstruction path.

\subsection{Reconstruction}
Training inputs are formed by Germano filtering and centered Fourier-mode truncation. The domain lengths are $L_x=L_y=50.176$, with DNS spacing $h=0.196$, an eightfold spatial refinement factor and filter length $\ell=1.568$. The JSON field named \texttt{alpha} records this filter length, not the physical HW adiabaticity. Input and output normalization are disabled for this run: their recorded means are zero and standard deviations are unity.

Before inversion, each predicted filtered field passes through a smooth spectral cutoff with multiplier
\begin{equation}
    Q(\mathbf{k})=\exp\left[-\left(\frac{|\mathbf{k}|}{k_c}\right)^8\right],
    \qquad k_c=0.707107\,\frac{\pi}{h}\simeq11.3339.
\end{equation}
The configured inverse is a truncated van Cittert reconstruction. For $g=Q\widetilde f_\theta$, implemented spectrally, it computes
\begin{equation}
    f^{(0)}=g,\qquad
    f^{(j+1)}=f^{(j)}+\omega\left[g-\mathcal{F}_\ell f^{(j)}\right],
    \quad j=0,\ldots,4,\qquad\omega=1.
    \label{eq:training_van_cittert}
\end{equation}
The reconstructed field is $f^{(5)}$ with its spatial mean removed. Thus this run uses five iterations of a regularized approximate inverse, rather than the exact differential inverse in Eq.~\eqref{eq:germano_inverse}. No post-inversion filter is enabled. Reconstructed vorticity is calculated from the potential using the second-order periodic finite-difference Laplacian,
\begin{equation}
    \zeta_\theta=\Delta_h\phi_\theta,\qquad
    (\Delta_h\phi)_{i,j}
    =\frac{\phi_{i+1,j}+\phi_{i-1,j}+\phi_{i,j+1}+\phi_{i,j-1}-4\phi_{i,j}}{h^2},
\end{equation}
with periodic indices. The DNS vorticity target is likewise derived from its potential.

The objective compares the reconstructed fields with DNS after cutoff, inversion and vorticity evaluation; gradients propagate through this entire path. It does not directly penalize the network's intermediate filtered output. Let
\begin{equation}
    e_n=\frac{n_\theta-n_{\mathrm{DNS}}}{s_n},\qquad
    e_\phi=\frac{\phi_\theta-\phi_{\mathrm{DNS}}}{s_\phi},\qquad
    e_\zeta=\frac{\zeta_\theta-\zeta_{\mathrm{DNS}}}{s_\zeta},
\end{equation}
where $s_n=s_\phi=1$ and $s_\zeta=1.2094048537$. The vorticity scaling remains active even though field normalization is disabled. With $\langle\cdot\rangle_{B,\Omega}$ denoting the mean over the batch and spatial grid, define
\begin{align}
    \mathcal{L}_{n\phi}^{(1)}
    &=\tfrac12\langle|e_n|+|e_\phi|\rangle_{B,\Omega},&
    \mathcal{L}_{n\phi}^{(2)}
    &=\tfrac12\langle e_n^2+e_\phi^2\rangle_{B,\Omega},\\
    \mathcal{L}_{\zeta}^{(1)}
    &=\langle|e_\zeta|\rangle_{B,\Omega},&
    \mathcal{L}_{\zeta}^{(2)}
    &=\langle e_\zeta^2\rangle_{B,\Omega}.
\end{align}
The implemented loss for this run is
\begin{align}
    \mathcal{L}
    &=\mathcal{L}_{n\phi}^{(1)}
      +\lambda_{\mathrm{mse}}\mathcal{L}_{n\phi}^{(2)}
      +\lambda_{\zeta}\left(\mathcal{L}_{\zeta}^{(1)}
      +\lambda_{\mathrm{mse}}\mathcal{L}_{\zeta}^{(2)}\right)\\
    &=\mathcal{L}_{n\phi}^{(1)}+0.1\mathcal{L}_{n\phi}^{(2)}
      +0.1\mathcal{L}_{\zeta}^{(1)}+0.01\mathcal{L}_{\zeta}^{(2)}.
    \label{eq:training_loss}
\end{align}
The density/potential terms average the two channels, so their unit prefactor is not a unit weight on each channel's separate loss. The squared-error contribution increases the penalty on larger residuals, while the vorticity terms constrain potential curvature through the derived field.

No adversarial loss, separate spectral loss or separate first-/second-derivative loss contributes to this objective. The nonzero vorticity loss nevertheless supplies a derivative-based constraint. The stored \texttt{variational=false} setting also excludes stochastic latent sampling.

The configuration specifies 100 epochs with batch size 4, using AdamW at a constant learning rate of $10^{-4}$, weight decay $10^{-6}$, moment coefficients $(0.9,0.999)$ and $\epsilon=10^{-8}$. Gradient norms are clipped to 1.0. Computation uses float64 precision on an NVIDIA H100 80GB GPU. A random split with seed 7 assigns 4,000 snapshots to training, 500 to validation and 500 to testing. Synchronized random periodic shifts augment the training fields while preserving alignment of inputs and targets. This snapshot split does not by itself establish temporal independence of the held-out data.

During LES integration, the current coarse fields are passed through the trained network and the configured regularized inverse. The potential determines vorticity, the reconstructed fields determine the fine-grid Poisson brackets, and Germano filtering followed by Fourier downsampling supplies the nonlinear terms to the coarse solver for time advancement.

\section{Results}\label{sec:Results}

The diagnostics below use the supplied U-Net validation plots for the four configurations. The numeric legend entries identify the coarse-grid sizes: 128 and 64 for the $256^2$ DNS cases, and 256, 128 and 64 for the $512^2$ DNS cases. DNS is the reference. These figures do not include a separately identified conventional LES closure or establish a comparison between adversarial and non-adversarial training. Accordingly, the plotted learned solutions are referred to as U-Net reconstructions in this section.

The following comparisons are qualitative assessments of the archived figures; pointwise error metrics, time-averaged transport errors and timing measurements require the underlying numerical records.

\subsection{Energy and enstrophy}

The continuum energy and generalized enstrophy are
\begin{align}
 E &= \frac12\int_\Omega\left(n^2+|\nabla\phi|^2\right)\,d\mathbf{x},\\
 W &= \frac12\int_\Omega(n-\zeta)^2\,d\mathbf{x}.
\end{align}
The supplied energy plots use the diagnostic convention
\begin{equation}
 E_{\mathrm{plot}}=\frac{L_xL_y}{2}\sum_{i,j}
 \left[n_{ij}^2+(D_x\phi)_{ij}^2+(D_y\phi)_{ij}^2\right],
\end{equation}
where $D_x$ and $D_y$ are fourth-order periodic finite-difference derivatives. This differs from the usual integral quadrature by the number of diagnostic grid points, $N_xN_y$, since $\Delta x\Delta y=L_xL_y/(N_xN_y)$. The enstrophy plots instead use
\begin{equation}
 W_{\mathrm{plot}}=\frac{\Delta x\Delta y}{2}\sum_{i,j}(n_{ij}-\zeta_{ij})^2.
\end{equation}
We retain the normalization of the supplied images. Absolute energy levels across diagnostic grids must therefore not be interpreted as directly comparable continuum integrals.

Figures~\ref{fig:energy} and~\ref{fig:enstrophy} show that the HW reconstructions retain the overall magnitude of both diagnostics, but do not track all DNS fluctuations. For HW\_N256, the 64-grid trajectory lies below DNS over substantial intervals, while the 128-grid trajectory also exhibits timing differences. For HW\_N512, the 64-grid solution has the clearest persistent deficit; the finer reconstructions remain closer but miss part of the late DNS increase.

For mHW\_N256, the initial decay is captured, but the subsequent recovery in energy and the enstrophy burst occur later in the reconstructions than in DNS. At late times, the reconstructed energy returns near the DNS level while the enstrophy remains substantially higher. Agreement in total energy alone therefore does not establish agreement in the density--vorticity diagnostic. In mHW\_N512, the 256- and 128-grid curves closely follow the energy and enstrophy decay over the shorter observation window, while the 64-grid curves show a larger deficit. This shorter window does not test the late evolution seen in mHW\_N256.

\subsection{Spectral statistics}

The archived spectra are shell sums of the total HW energy, including both density and potential-gradient contributions, rather than kinetic energy alone:
\begin{equation}
 E(k_s)=\frac12\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathcal{S}_s}
 \left(|\widehat n_{\mathbf{k}}|^2+|\mathbf{k}|^2|\widehat\phi_{\mathbf{k}}|^2\right),
\end{equation}
where the Fourier coefficients are normalized by $N_xN_y$ and $\mathcal{S}_s$ denotes a radial wavenumber shell. This spectral normalization is distinct from that of $E_{\mathrm{plot}}$.

Figure~\ref{fig:spectra} includes all three available initial/final spectrum images. No corresponding initial/final image is present for HW\_N512, so no final-spectrum conclusion is drawn for that case. Initial spectra nearly overlap over the energetic scales. At the final time, HW\_N256 retains a similar spectral shape, with differences at the lowest wavenumbers and in the dissipative tail. For mHW\_N256, the reconstructions retain more high-wavenumber energy than DNS, consistent with the elevated late-time enstrophy. For mHW\_N512, agreement is close over the energetic scales, but the reconstructed tails depart from DNS at high wavenumbers. The vertical reference marks the 64-grid Nyquist wavenumber, not a measured boundary of accurate reconstruction.

\subsection{Transport diagnostics}

The archived particle-flux histories use the non-zonal density $n'=n-\langle n\rangle_y$ and the velocities
\begin{equation}
 v_x=-\partial_y\phi,\qquad v_y=\partial_x\phi.
\end{equation}
The plotted quantities are domain-averaged turbulent fluxes,
\begin{equation}
 \overline\Gamma_x=-\langle n'\partial_y\phi\rangle_{x,y},\qquad
 \overline\Gamma_y=\langle n'\partial_x\phi\rangle_{x,y}.
\end{equation}
Here the prime denotes removal of the poloidal mean, distinct from the LES spatial filter denoted by a tilde elsewhere. The poloidal flux therefore excludes transport by the zonal density component.

Figure~\ref{fig:particle-flux} shows positive radial flux throughout the plotted intervals. In the HW cases, the reconstructions preserve its overall scale, but the coarsest solution generally underpredicts it and the temporal fluctuations differ from DNS. For mHW\_N256, the initial radial-flux decay is reproduced, while the subsequent burst is delayed, in parallel with the energy and enstrophy transients. The mHW\_N512 radial-flux decay is followed more closely over the available interval. Poloidal flux is smaller and changes sign; its detailed timing is less consistently reproduced, particularly after the initial transient. These results support qualitative transport agreement in some intervals, but not a uniform error bound or a quantified temporal correlation.

\subsection{Computational performance}

Wall-clock runtimes and MPI task counts were extracted from the final \texttt{Run time} entry and the \texttt{Processor number} entry, respectively, in each \texttt{BOUT\_runs/HW\_*/data/BOUT.log.0} file. Each LES run is compared with the DNS run at its corresponding reference resolution. The completed DNS runs cover $\Delta t_{\mathrm{DNS}}=2000$ simulation time units, whereas the completed LES runs cover $\Delta t_{\mathrm{LES}}=1500$. We therefore report both the raw runtime ratio and a speedup normalized by simulated duration:
\begin{equation}
    S_{\mathrm{raw}}=\frac{T_{\mathrm{DNS}}}{T_{\mathrm{LES}}},
    \qquad
    S_{\mathrm{wall}}=
    \frac{T_{\mathrm{DNS}}/\Delta t_{\mathrm{DNS}}}
         {T_{\mathrm{LES}}/\Delta t_{\mathrm{LES}}},
    \qquad
    S_{\mathrm{MPI}}=S_{\mathrm{wall}}
    \frac{P_{\mathrm{DNS}}}{P_{\mathrm{LES}}},
\end{equation}
where $T$ is wall-clock runtime and $P$ is the number of MPI tasks. Here $S_{\mathrm{MPI}}$ measures the reduction in MPI task-time per unit simulated time; it is not a measured serial speedup. Values greater than unity indicate an improvement. Duration normalization compares average costs over the recorded trajectories rather than timings over an identical simulation interval.

\section{Discussion}

The supplied results assess the evolution of reconstructed-field diagnostics; they do not compare against direct prediction of sub-grid terms. Physical consistency remains an important constraint on the reconstruction. In particular, calculating vorticity from the generated electrostatic potential enforces the elliptic relationship
\[
\zeta=\nabla^2\phi
\]
and prevents independent-field drift.

The modified $256^2$ case highlights a limitation of relying on energy alone: near-DNS energy can coexist with excess enstrophy, altered PDFs and delayed transport bursts. The closer agreement of the modified $512^2$ case applies to a much shorter interval and does not establish improved long-time stability with increasing resolution.

The approach has several limitations. First, the generator may produce nonphysical small-scale structures outside the training distribution. Second, long-time stability is not guaranteed solely by minimizing instantaneous reconstruction error. Finally, the present method is limited by the validity of the chosen filter and by the statistical coverage of the training dataset.

\section{Conclusions}\label{sec:Conclusions}

A generative-AI closure for edge-plasma LES has been developed by learning DNS fields filtered with a Germano-consistent procedure. The reconstructed fields are used to evaluate the nonlinear Poisson-bracket terms, while the vorticity is obtained directly from the generated electrostatic potential.

The method was tested with the HW\_N256, HW\_N512, mHW\_N256 and mHW\_N512 configurations. The main conclusions are:

\begin{itemize}
    \item the generative model reconstructs the dominant spatial structures of the DNS fields;
    \item the learned fields retain several large-scale statistical features, while high-wavenumber spectral tails and late-time mHW\_N256 enstrophy show discrepancies;
    \item radial-flux trends are reproduced in several intervals, but burst timing and poloidal-flux fluctuations differ from DNS;
    \item the completed HW runs reduce MPI task-time per unit simulated time by factors of 5.03--185.37, with a duration-normalized wall-clock speedup of 1.45 for HW\_DNS512\_LES64;
    \item the mHW\_N256 trajectory exhibits pronounced late-time discrepancies; unequal observation windows preclude a controlled resolution ranking.
\end{itemize}

Future work will investigate parameter-conditioned generators, explicit enforcement of conservation laws, uncertainty quantification and extension to fully three-dimensional edge-plasma models such as Hermes-3.
